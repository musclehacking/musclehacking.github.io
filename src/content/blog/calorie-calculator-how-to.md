<p>This page breaks down how to use the <a href="/calorie-calculator/" target="_blank" title="Calorie and Macro Calculator" rel="noopener">calorie calculator web application</a> to work out your daily
    calories and macronutrients.</p>
<p>The calculator allows you to forecast your estimated weight change per week based on your calorie
    deficit/surplus. You can also adjust your protein, fat, and carbohydrate macros as desired. </p>
<p>Your <a href="#bmr" title="What does BMR mean?">BMR</a> and <a href="#tdee" title="What does TDEE mean?">TDEE</a> are displayed for convenience.</p>
<h3 id="stats">Stats</h3>
<p id="start-p">Here you input the following basic personal information:</p>
<ul>
    <li>Age (in years)</li>
    <li>Weight (in kilograms or pounds)</li>
    <li>Height (in centimetres or inches)</li>
    <li>Gender (male or female)</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="modifiers">Modifiers</h3>
<h4 id="activity-level">Activity Level</h4>
<p>Your <strong>Activity Level</strong> serves as a multiplier on top of your <a title="BMR Defined" target="_blank" href="/blog/calorie-calculator-how-to#bmr" rel="noopener"><strong>BMR</strong></a> (Basal Metabolic
    Rate). The result of this calculation is called your <a title="TDEE Defined" target="_blank" href="/blog/calorie-calculator-how-to#tdee" rel="noopener"><strong>TDEE</strong></a> (Total Daily Energy
    Expenditure).</p>
<p>Think of your BMR as the energy you would need to keep alive if you were in a coma. Energy required for
    processes like breathing, blood circulation, controlling body temperature, cell growth, etc.</p>
<p>What you care about though, is your TDEE. This is the <strong><em>total amount of energy you need each
            day to maintain your body weight</em></strong> after you factor in all of your activity.</p>
<p>This is not the same as your BMR, as even the least active among us aren’t lying motionless in bed all
    day.</p>
<p>The Activity Level modifiers are:</p>
<ul>
    <li><strong>Sedentary:</strong> Little or no exercise, office job (1.2×)</li>
    <li><strong>Lightly Active:</strong> Light daily activity &amp; exercise 1-3 days per week
        (1.375×)</li>
    <li><strong>Moderately Active:</strong> Moderate daily activity &amp; exercise 3-5 days per week
        (1.55×)</li>
    <li><strong>Very Active:</strong> Physically demanding lifestyle &amp; exercise 6-7 days per week
        (1.725×)</li>
    <li><strong>Extremely Active:</strong> Hard daily exercise/sports &amp; physical job (1.9×)</li>
</ul>
<p>Be warned: <strong>most people tend to overestimate their activity level</strong>. </p>
<p>This is usually due to them basing their activity level on their number of gym sessions per week. This is
    not the right way to go about it.</p>
<p>Your activity level is primarily based off of what you do <strong><em>outside</em></strong> the gym. Four
    1 hour strength training sessions at the gym each week is less than 2 hours of actual activity. It does
    <strong><em>not</em></strong> result in a large number of calories burnt.
</p>
<p>Conversely, a teacher on her feet for the lions share of the week is most likely <em>“Lightly
        Active”</em>. The intermittent cardio throughout the day adds up.</p>
<p>If your only activity outside the office is the gym, put yourself down as <em>“Sedentary”</em>. You
    <em>might</em> be slightly over depending on your intensity and frequency – but more likely you won’t
    be. You can adjust your estimate based on the first couple week’s progress.
</p>
<p>If you think you’re on the line between levels and it could go either way – go with the lower estimate.
</p>
<h4 id="goal">Goal</h4>
<p>This dropdown provides an easy way to match your qualitative weight loss/gain goal with a quantitative
    value. The options are: </p>
<ul>
    <li>Lose Weight (–20%)</li>
    <li>Slowly Lose Weight (–10%)</li>
    <li>Maintain Weight (0%)</li>
    <li>Slowly Gain Weight (+10%)</li>
    <li>Gain Weight (+20%)</li>
</ul>
<p>The number in the proceeding brackets specifies the caloric deficit/surplus. For example, (+10%) means
    that your daily calorie goal will be 10% higher than your <a href="#tdee" target="_blank" title="What does TDEE mean?" rel="noopener">TDEE</a>.</p>
<p>Alternatively, you can input a specific calorie goal in the <strong>Calorie Deficit/Surplus</strong>
    section.</p>
<h4 id="how-much-protein">How Much Protein?</h4>
<p>Here there are 3 standardised options:</p>
<ul id="ul-nm7">
    <li>1g per pound</li>
    <li>0.82g per pound</li>
    <li>1.5g per pound</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>1g per pound</strong> is the standard and recommended option. This provides you with enough
    protein to facilitate muscle growth <strong><em>with a large margin of error</em></strong>.</p>
<p><strong>0.82g per pound</strong> still provides enough protein to facilitate muscle growth. Think of this
    as a lower bar for the amount of protein you should be consuming. Although the subject of hot debate,
    current science points to 0.82g protein/pound being <strong><em>enough</em></strong> protein. </p>
<p>Although it’s <em>unlikely</em> additional protein will result in increased gains, you still may want to
    consider a higher protein intake as:</p>
<ul>
    <li>Protein is a more satiating macronutrient <a title="The effects of high protein diets on thermogenesis, satiety and weight loss: a critical review" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmedhealth/PMH0021577/" rel="noopener">[1]</a></li>
    <li>A higher protein intake restricts your food choices, forcing you to eat less junk</li>
    <li>The TEF (Thermic Effect of Food) means protein is closer to 3.2 kcal per gram, not 4 kcal per gram
        <a title="A perspective on food energy standards for nutrition labelling." target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/11299073" rel="noopener">[2]</a>
    </li>
</ul>
<p>The above reasoning is why you might even want to consider <strong>1.5g per pound</strong>. </p>
<p>If you’re an expert in going way too far with your cheat meals to the point of ruining your diet – 1.5g
    per pound is for you.</p>
<h4 id="fat-carb-calorie-split">Fat/Carb Calorie Split</h4>
<p>This is the percentage split (<em>calorie-wise</em>) between fat and carbs <em>after</em> you account for
    your protein intake.</p>
<p>You have a good amount of leeway in your distribution between carbs and fat. <em><strong>The split
            between carbs and fat is largely a matter of personal preference</strong></em>. So long as
    you’re not on the extreme of either side, there is no point stressing about the allocation.
</p>
<p>Some of us feel better with more carbs and less fat, and some of us feel better with more fat and less
    carbs. In general the more active you are, the more likely you’ll suit a higher carb intake.</p>
<p>As a rule of thumb, <strong>you’ll want to consume no less than 0.25g fat per pound, or approximately
        0.5g fat per kilogram</strong>. </p>
<p>Although this may seem like a low bar, it can be broken when you’re on a hard cut and hence don’t have
    that many calories to work with. Getting less than this amount of fat for an elongated period of time
    can easily lead to dry skin and/or hair. Would not recommend.</p>
<h3 id="results">Results</h3>
<h4 id="bmr">BMR</h4>
<p>Stands for your <strong>Basal Metabolic Rate</strong>.</p>
<p>This is essentially the amount of energy you would need to keep alive if you were in a coma. Energy
    required for processes like breathing, blood circulation, controlling body temperature, cell growth,
    etc.</p>
<p>This calculation is based on your Age, Weight, Height, and Gender. It is then multiplied by your <a href="#activity-level" target="_blank" title="How do I determine my Activity Level?" rel="noopener"><strong>Activity Level</strong></a> to give your TDEE.
</p>
<h4 id="tdee">TDEE</h4>
<p>Stands for your <strong>Total Daily Energy Expenditure</strong>. Also known as your “<em>maintenance
        calories</em>”.</p>
<p>Your TDEE is the <strong><em>total amount of energy you need each day to maintain your body
            weight</em></strong> after you factor in all of your activity.</p>
<p>So in an ideal world where you eat your exact TDEE each day, you would maintain the same body weight.</p>
<p>In reality this never happens due to the variance in our daily activity, the exact amount of food we eat,
    and water weight fluctuations.</p>
<p>Nevertheless, getting a ballpark figure of your TDEE is all you really need to get going. You can track
    your progress against your estimate and readjust as required.</p>
<h4 id="daily-calories-and-macros">Daily Calories and Macros</h4>
<p>This is final result of all of your input data. It breaks down the Calories, Protein, Fat, and
    Carbohydrates you should eat each day to reach your physique goals.</p>
<p><strong>Do not stress about small deviations from these numbers</strong>. A ±5% difference will not
    affect your physique. </p>
<p>Why? Each of these numbers have error built in. Even the lower protein intake of 0.82g per pound was
    based on a 95% confidence interval. </p>
<p><strong>All calorie counters are approximations.</strong> Regardless of how many decimal places worth of
    carbs MyFitnessPal tells you are in your 160.8 g apple – it’s only an estimate. </p>
<p>Use the calculated values as a solid approximation. Adjust your estimate as required based off of your
    weight loss/gain during the first few weeks of your diet. If you need help with this, ask. I plan on
    making a tool to help with this in the near future. </p>
<h4 id="estimated-weight-loss-per-week">Estimated Weight Loss per Week</h4>
<p>This estimate is based on the ~3500 calories in one pound of fat.</p>
<p>E.g. A 500 calorie deficit per day is around 1 pound of fat loss per week.</p>
<p>So what kind of weight gain/loss should you aim for? The below serves as a <strong><em>practical and
            realistic</em></strong> expectation for a 6 to 12 month program. </p>
<p><strong>Fat Loss with Minimal Muscle Loss</strong></p>
<ul id="ul-nm1">
    <li>2 pounds per week in the obese (BMI of 30 or above) </li>
    <li>1 pound per week in the overweight (BMI of 25 to 30)</li>
    <li>½ pound per week, or less, in guys who are lean and trying to get even leaner </li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Muscle Gain with Minimal Fat Gain</strong></p>
<ul id="ul-nm2">
    <li>2 to 3 pounds per month in novices and advanced beginners (those with less than 2 years of
        consistent strength training)</li>
    <li>1 to 2 pounds per month in intermediates (those with 2 to 4 years of consistent training) </li>
    <li>½ pound per month in advanced lifters who are close to their genetic ceiling</li>
</ul>
<p>It’s important to note that <strong><em>the first few weeks and months will bring the biggest
            losses/gains</em></strong>. </p>
<p>For example, it’s common to drop a full 2kg in your first week of cutting – especially if you lowered
    your carb intake. This is great for motivation, but it’s not a realistic representation of what you
    should expect long term. </p>
<p>This exaggerated change is primarily due to changes in water weight, as well as the initial shock your
    body experiences from the abrupt change in calorie intake. </p>
<p><strong>What this means</strong> is that you shouldn’t fret over your weight loss/gains plateauing after
    the first few weeks. Rather, you should expect it. Don’t let it mess with your motivation.</p>
<h3 id="the-leangains-method">The Leangains Method Calculator</h3>
<p>The following information is specific to the <em>Leangains</em> <a href="/calorie-calculator/?leangains" target="_blank" title="Leangains Calorie and Macro Calculator" rel="noopener">calorie and macronutrient
        calculator</a> (<em>select “Leangains” in the “Diet” dropdown</em>).</p>
<p>This calculator uses the equation recommended by <a title="About: Martin Berkhan" target="_blank" href="https://leangains.com/about-me/" rel="noopener">Martin Berkhan</a> in his book <a title="The Leangains Method" target="_blank" href="http://www.eatstopeat.org/introduction-to-the-leangains-method/" rel="noopener">The Leangains Method</a> to
    estimate your daily calorie and macronutrient needs.</p>
<p>To use this approach to calculate your <a href="#tdee" target="_blank" title="What does TDEE mean?" rel="noopener">TDEE</a>, you multiply your body weight in kilograms by your calculated
    <em>base value</em>. This base value starts at 28 for men, and 26 for women.
</p>
<p>Your base value is modified depending on your <em>Stats</em> – age, height, body fat, muscle mass, and
    steps taken each day.</p>
<p>Let’s use our imaginary friend Paul as an example. Paul’s stats are: </p>
<ul>
    <li>21 year old male (+0.5)</li>
    <li>13% body fat (+0)</li>
    <li>180 cm tall (+0)</li>
    <li>Muscular (+0.5)</li>
    <li>8000 steps per day (+1.0)</li>
</ul>
<p>So Paul’s final base value is → <em>28 + 0.5 + 0 + 0 + 0.5 + 1.0 = 30</em>. Paul weighs in at 80
    kg, so that puts him at: </p>
<blockquote>
    <p>80 kg × 30 base value = 2400 calorie <a class="no-ul" href="#tdee" title="What does TDEE mean?">TDEE</a></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Below I explain the specifics of how each factor affects your base value. Note that the <a href="/calorie-calculator/?leangains" target="_blank" title="Leangains Calorie and Macro Calculator" rel="noopener">calorie calculator</a> automatically does this math
    for you. This is just an explanation of the logic behind the calculator, for those interested.</p>
<h4 id="age">Age</h4>
<p>Your age alters your base value in the following way:</p>
<ul id="ul-nm8">
    <li>&lt;25 years old: +0.5</li>
    <li>25-45 years old: 0</li>
    <li>&gt;45 years old: –0.5</li>
</ul>
<p>So an 18 year old man/woman would add 0.5 to their base value.</p>
<h4 id="height">Height</h4>
<p>How your height alters your base value depends on your gender:</p>
<p><strong>For Men</strong> </p>
<ul id="ul-nm9">
    <li>&lt;167 cm: –1</li>
    <li>167-185 cm: 0</li>
    <li>&gt;185 cm: +1</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
    <p>167 cm ≈ 5 feet, 5 inches = 65 inches</p>
    <p>185 cm ≈ 6 feet, 1 inches = 73 inches</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>For Women</strong> </p>
<ul id="ul-nm10">
    <li>&lt;153 cm: –1</li>
    <li>153-170 cm: 0</li>
    <li>&gt;170 cm: +1</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
    <p>153 cm ≈ 5 feet, 0 inches = 60 inches</p>
    <p>170 cm ≈ 5 feet, 7 inches = 67 inches</p>
</blockquote>
<p>So a 175 cm tall guy would keep his base value the same, while a 175 cm tall woman would add 1 to her
    base value.</p>
<h4 id="body-fat">Body Fat</h4>
<p>How your body fat percentage alters your base value depends on your gender:</p>
<p><strong>For Men</strong> </p>
<ul id="ul-nm3">
    <li>&lt;10% body fat: +0.5 </li>
    <li>11-19% body fat: 0</li>
    <li>20-24% body fat: –0.5 </li>
    <li>25-29% body fat: –1.5</li>
    <li>30-34% body fat: –2.5</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>For Women</strong> </p>
<ul id="ul-nm4">
    <li>&lt;18% body fat: +0.5 </li>
    <li>19-27% body fat: 0</li>
    <li>28-32% body fat: –0.5 </li>
    <li>33-37% body fat: –1.5</li>
    <li>38-42% body fat: –2.5</li>
</ul>
<p>So a man with 22% body fat would subtract 1.5 from his base value. Whereas a woman with 25% body fat
    would keep her base value the same.</p>
<h4 id="muscle-mass">Muscle Mass</h4>
<p>Your muscle mass alters your base value in the following way:</p>
<ul id="ul-nm5">
    <li>Standard: 0</li>
    <li>Muscular: +0.5</li>
    <li>Very Muscular: +1</li>
</ul>
<p>Note that <em>Very Muscular</em> is only applicable to males. Martin recommends that those with a
    Fat-Free Mass Index (FFMI) &gt;22 choose <em>Muscular</em>. For <em>Very Muscular</em>, your FFMI should
    be &gt;24. </p>
<p>There is no FFMI recommendation for women – women can use <em>Muscular</em> at their own discretion.</p>
<h4 id="goal-lg">Goal (Leangains specific)</h4>
<p>Your goal calories deviate from your <a href="#tdee" target="_blank" title="What does TDEE mean?" rel="noopener">TDEE</a> depending on your gender, and whether you’re cutting or
    bulking. Either way it is a constant number (<em>as opposed to a percentage</em>).</p>
<p>For men, a ±500 calorie deficit/surplus depending on whether you’re cutting or bulking. </p>
<p>For women, a ±350 calorie deficit/surplus depending on whether you’re cutting or bulking. </p>
<p>This will result in an approximate weight loss/gain of 0.5 kilograms (1.1 pounds) for men, or 0.35
    kilograms (0.77 pounds) for women.</p>
<h4 id="steps">Steps</h4>
<p>This is an approximation for the number of steps you take each day. If you want an accurate result, use a
    pedometer or phone application such as Health (iOS) or Google Fit (Android). </p>
<p>If you’re sedentary for most of your week or are unsure, assume a value of 5000 steps (<em>note that
        steps less than 6000 have the same activity multiplier</em>). </p>
<p>Your base value is altered as follows: </p>
<ul id="ul-nm6">
    <li>&lt;6000 steps: 0 </li>
    <li>6000 – 7500 steps: +0.5 </li>
    <li>Add another +0.5 for every 1250 step increment above 7500 steps </li>
</ul>
<p>For example, someone taking 9000 steps each day would add 1.5 to their base value.</p>
<h4 id="how-much-protein-lg">How much Protein? (Leangains specific)</h4>
<p><a title="The Leangains Method" target="_blank" href="https://leangains.com/the-leangains-method-is-here/" rel="noopener">The Leangains Method</a> recommends
    getting as close to 60% of your calorie intake from protein as you can. This
    <strong><em>extremely</em></strong> high protein recommendation is to reap benefits <em>outside</em> of
    pure muscle building. Namely protein’s high TEF (Thermic Effect of Food), as well as the added
    difficulty of making poor food choices when you’re eating a high protein diet.
</p>
<p>Martin himself finds 55% protein to be his sweet spot. He notes that if you find 60% protein too
    restrictive then you can drop it down to 50% – which he believes is doable. </p>
<p>If you still think this protein intake is too high, then consider switching the “<em>Diet</em>” dropdown
    of the <a href="/calorie-calculator/" target="_blank" title="Calorie and Macro Calculator" rel="noopener">calorie
        calculator</a> to “<em>Standard</em>” and selecting a lower protein intake of 0.82g/pound or
    1g/pound. This gives you more leeway with your other macros while still provides enough protein for
    muscle building. More information on protein intakes <a href="#how-much-protein" target="_blank" title="How much Protein?" rel="noopener">here</a>.</p>
