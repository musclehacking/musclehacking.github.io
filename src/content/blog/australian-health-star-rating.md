<p>The Australian Government has come up with a fantastic new way to keep their taxpayers trim.</p>
<p>It’s ingenious. </p>
<p>They label each food with a <strong><em>health star rating</em></strong> out of 5. </p>
<p>The full 5 stars is reserved for only the most audacious of health foods. Think 17-Grain-Gluten-Free quinoa.
</p>
<p>Eating healthy has never been so easy. 6 pack abs is now within reach to anyone that can count to 5. </p>
<p>Maccas might as well pack up shop now. Colonel Sanders’ reign is over. Long live the standing desk. The <a href="https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports-statistics/behaviours-risk-factors/overweight-obesity/overview" target="_blank" title="Obesity in Australia" rel="noopener">Australian obesity epidemic</a> is over – <em>Right?</em></p>
<p>There are many complexities and nuances to nutrition. So did the government really succeed in simplifying this
  complexity to a star rating out of 5?</p>
<p>.... <strong>nope</strong>.</p>

  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/pickle-heath-star.jpg" alt="Pickle Rick Boom Big Reveal" width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" decoding="async">

<p>What does a food being “<em>healthy</em>” mean to you? </p>
<p>Although you can point to some foods you would consider healthy, you might find defining it to be a challenge.
  You would be in close company.</p>
<p>If you were to ask 10 different people, you would get 10 different definitions. These explanations are often
  quite hand-wavey, prompting further questions (what exactly does “<em>good for you</em>” mean Stacey?).</p>
<p>And that’s exactly what the Australian Health Star Rating does. Prompt further questions. Like “<em>who in
    their right mind would advocate such terrible nutritional advice</em>”.</p>
<p>But we’re getting ahead of ourselves. Just what is a “Health Star Rating”? According to the <a href="http://healthstarrating.gov.au/internet/healthstarrating/publishing.nsf/Content/About-health-stars" target="_blank" title="What is a Health Star Rating?" rel="noopener">Health Star Rating website</a>:</p>
<blockquote>
  <p><em>“The Health Star Rating is a front-of-pack labelling system that rates the overall nutritional profile of
      packaged food and assigns it a rating from ½ a star to 5 stars. It provides a quick, easy, standard way to
      compare similar packaged foods. The more stars, the healthier the choice.”</em> </p>
</blockquote>
<p>You read right – <strong>The more stars, the healthier the choice</strong>. It turns out that the nuances of
  nutrition were all in our head! The government managed to compact these complexities into 5 simple stars. </p>

<p>Huh? That doesn’t seem right. Didn’t more stars mean more healthy? </p>
<p>Beer batter fries aren’t usually what comes to mind when we think of health foods. And isn’t Milo just sugar?
</p>
<p>Well, remember this part of the Health Star definition: </p>
<blockquote>
  <p><em>“...It provides a quick, easy, standard way to <strong>compare similar packaged foods</strong>.”</em>
  </p>
</blockquote>
<p>Similar. Packaged. Foods. Oh. So we’re not comparing chicken breast to quinoa. <strong><em>We’re comparing beer
      battered fries to chilli cheese fries</em></strong>. That’s not useful, that’s just misleading.</p>
<p>And what is a “<em>similar packaged food</em>” exactly? Are we comparing breakfast cereals with other breakfast
  choices – like eggs or oats – or only against other breakfast cereals. Is the relative “<em>healthiness</em>” of
  <a href="https://www.kelloggs.com.au/en_AU/brands/coco-pops.html" target="_blank" title="Kellogg‘s Coco Pops" rel="noopener">Coco Pops</a> vs <a href="https://www.kelloggs.com.au/en_AU/brands/froot-loops.html" target="_blank" title="Kellogg‘s Fruit Loops" rel="noopener">Fruit Loops</a> useful to anyone with more than 3 brain cells?
</p>
<p>So what is a “<em>similar packaged food</em>” that we’re comparing Milo to exactly... Nesquik?</p>
<picture class="intrinsic intrinsic--16x9">
  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/nesquik-heath-star.jpg" alt="Nesquik Health Star Rating" width="800" height="450" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</picture>
<p class="figurecap"> Nesquik – ?? Health Stars </p>
<p>Looks like Nesquik doesn’t have a Health Star Rating. And herein lies another problem with Health Stars – <a href="http://healthstarrating.gov.au/internet/healthstarrating/publishing.nsf/Content/About-health-stars" target="_blank" title="Who developed the Health Star Rating system?" rel="noopener"><strong><em>it’s a voluntary rating
        system</em></strong></a>. That’s right. Don’t like your dodgy rating? No worries – just don’t show it!
</p>
<p>This still doesn’t explain how chocolate-coated sugar (Milo) got a 4.5 star health rating. Even compared to
  deep-fried ice cream Milo doesn’t deserve 4.5 stars. </p>
<p>Well lucky for Milo, there is an “<em>as prepared</em>” rule. This allows companies to rate products based on
  their suggested recipe for preparation rather than their standalone nutritional value. And how do we prepare
  Milo? We <del>inject it directly into our blood stream</del> have it with a 200ml glass of skim milk. </p>
<p>Milo + Skim Milk = 4.5 stars. If Milo’s healthiness was based on its own merits, <a href="http://www.abc.net.au/radio/programs/am/food-industry-stalling-on-health-star-rule-review/9032848" target="_blank" title="Food industry stalling on health star rule review" rel="noopener">it would get 1.5 stars.</a> </p>
<picture class="intrinsic intrinsic--70">
  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/milo-skim-milk-health-star.jpg" alt="Nesquik Health Star Rating" width="1200" height="840" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</picture>
<p class="figurecap"> The Magic of “<em>As Prepared</em>” </p>
<p>Since skim milk is <a href="http://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/Lookup/by%20Subject/4364.0.55.007~2011-12~Main%20Features~Milk%20products%20and%20dishes~721" target="_blank" title="Australian Health Survey - Milk Products and Dishes" rel="noopener">only 11% of consumed dairy
    milk</a>, that’s <em>probably</em> not how the Milo was prepared. Not to forget the people who forgo the milk
  altogether. Anything from “<a href="http://thesmartlocal.com/read/milo-recipes" target="_blank" title="How People Really Eat Milo" rel="noopener"><em>Easy Fried Milo French Toast</em></a>” to taking it straight from the
  spoon (<em>like a normal person</em>).</p>
<p>By that logic sugar deserves at least a 4 Health Star Rating. After all, it is <em>intended</em> to be “<em>as
    prepared</em>” in a coffee with skim milk. </p>
<p>Coffee + skim milk + sugar = 4 stars. Now I can stick a big “<em>4 Health Star Rating</em>” label on my sugar,
  and still sleep well at night knowing that my product has more nutritional integrity than Milo.</p>
<p>Arguably the best part about the new health star rating is its direct contradiction to other government
  recommendations. The <a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines" target="_blank" title="Australian Dietary Guidelines" rel="noopener">Australian Dietary Guidelines</a> takes a whole-foods approach to
  dieting, encouraging Australians to eat a wide variety of nutritious foods. It even <a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines/about-australian-dietary-guidelines" target="_blank" title="How do I make healthy food choices?" rel="noopener">calls out the bad tendency fad-diet’s have</a> of unnecessarily
  strict diet restrictions (<em>cough</em> Paleo Diet <em>cough</em>): </p>
<blockquote>
  <p>“The advice focuses on dietary patterns that promote health and wellbeing rather than recommending that you
    eat – or completely avoid – specific foods.”</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That actually sounds reasonable. The infographic is pretty good too: </p>
<picture class="intrinsic intrinsic--3">
  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/australian-guide-to-healthy-eating.jpg" alt="The Australian Guide to Healthy Eating" width="754" height="952" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</picture>
<p class="figurecap"> The Australian Guide to Healthy Eating </p>
<p>Definitely not perfect (<em>the biggest section should <strong>not</strong> be grain foods</em>), but
  reasonable. </p>
<p>In contrast, the Health Star Rating system bases its healthiness on the nutritional composition of the food.
  Give <a href="http://healthstarrating.gov.au/internet/healthstarrating/publishing.nsf/Content/online-calculator#/step/1" target="_blank" title="Health Star Rating Online Calculator" rel="noopener">the online calculator</a> a product’s energy,
  saturated fat, total sugars, sodium, protein, fibre, and % fruit and vegetable content, and it will spit out a
  star rating. Because <em>that’s all that matters</em> when it comes to eating healthy! </p>
<p>These two rating systems take a very different approach to evaluating the “<em>healthiness</em>” of a food.
  Notice in the above infographic our beloved 4 star beer batter fries receive the worst rating of “<em>Only
    sometimes and in small amounts</em>”. Whoops. </p>
<p>Nutrition can be confusing enough on its own without the government contradicting itself. The point of the
  rating system was to provide a simple way for people to understand how “<em>healthy</em>” a product is. </p>
<p>It was targeted at those who – whether due to time restrictions, different priorities, or confusion caused by
  the hundreds of conflicting opinions – are in the dark when it comes to nutrition.</p>
<p>The target market is certainly not the type to read the fine print on the back of a box. Or go onto the Health
  Star Rating website to understand the details of how the stars are allocated. </p>
<p>They’re the type to quickly count the number of stars on the label and use that as an indication for how
  healthy the product is. This seems to have been the <em>intent</em> of the Heath Star Rating system, it just got
  lost in the nonsense of “<em>as prepared</em>” and “<em>similar packaged food</em>”. Now the system is fanning
  the already hot flames of confusion that surround nutrition.</p>
<p>In short: the Australian Health Star Rating system is a castle in the air, connected to the ground by thin
  strings of nutritional science. </p>
<p>Don’t rely on it for anything other than a good laugh.</p>
