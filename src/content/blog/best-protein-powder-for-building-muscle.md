<p>You’re going to supplement your diet with more protein. Fantastic.</p>

<p>Whey protein. Casein protein. Pea protein. Rice protein. Apple-pie protein. </p>
<p>Okay, the last one isn’t a real thing (<em>yet</em>). But there is definitely no shortage of exotically named
  protein supplements. </p>
<p>So what’s the best protein powder for muscle gain? </p>

<h5 id="type-of-protein">The Type Of Protein</h5>
<p>The type of protein means either a <strong><em>completely different source of protein</em></strong> or the
  <strong><em>same source but refined to a different extent</em></strong>.
</p>
<p>An example of a <strong><em>completely different source</em></strong> is whey protein vs wheat protein. Whey
  protein is derived from milk. Wheat protein is derived from wheat. These are fundamentally different sources of
  protein.</p>
<p>The process of extracting protein from milk is different from the process of extracting protein from wheat.
  Part of the final cost of the protein powder will depend on how costly it is to refine and isolate the protein
  from the source.</p>
<p><strong>More refinement → More costly.</strong></p>
<p>There is a point where it is no longer economical to further refine a protein source to get a higher percentage
  yield of protein. This is reflected in the final protein content of different sources of protein.</p>
<p>It’s why you see rice and hemp protein (generally) having lower protein content than whey protein. Extracting
  whey protein from milk is cheaper than extracting the equivalent amount of protein from rice or hemp. </p>
<picture class="intrinsic intrinsic--new2">
  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/what-is-hemp-protein.jpg" alt="Pot of hemp seeds with a hemp leaf placed on top." width="700" height="392" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</picture>
<p class="figurecap"> Hemp protein is made from hemp seeds. Yes – weed. </p>
<p>An example of the <strong><em>same source refined to a different extent</em></strong> is Whey Protein Isolate
  vs Whey Protein Concentrate. </p>
<p>Both Whey Protein Isolate (WPI) and Whey Protein Concentrate (WPC) are derived from milk. The source of protein
  is the same. </p>
<p>The difference here is the refinement process. You get whey protein by pushing milk through a filter, removing
  the curd (<em>that’s your casein protein</em>). Let the whey protein dry, and you’re left with your WPC. </p>
<p>If you keep processing and refining your WPC – removing more lactose and fat – you get WPI. </p>
<p><strong><em>WPI is just a more refined version of WPC.</em></strong></p>
<p>As you would expect, removal of lactose and fat results in WPI having a higher protein content than WPC,
  regardless of the supplier.</p>
<h5 id="brand-of-protein">The Brand Of Protein</h5>
<p>The brand of a protein powder comes into play when you’re comparing the same protein from two different
  suppliers.</p>
<p>For example, <strong><em>Peter’s Protein</em></strong> and <strong><em>Steve’s Supplements</em></strong> both
  sell Whey Protein Concentrate (WPC).</p>
<p>But Peter’s WPC has 85% protein, while Steve’s WPC only has 70% protein.</p>
<p>This is a common occurrence: there is a large variation in protein content between suppliers, even for the same
  type of protein powder.</p>
<p>This variation depends on the amount of filler and flavouring they put into the protein, as well as their
  refinement process. </p>
<p><strong>This can vary a lot</strong>. If you’ve compared the nutrition label of a few brands of WPC, you’ll
  know exactly what I mean.</p>
<p>This is why it’s important to look deeper than just the type of protein. The brand matters too – not all
  suppliers were made equal. The best protein powders often have significantly higher protein content than
  inferior products on the market.</p>
<h4 id="amino-acid">Amino Acid Content</h4>
<p>Amino acids are the building blocks of protein. They come in two forms: </p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Unessential Amino Acids</strong> are amino acids naturally made by your body. 11 of the 20 amino
    acids are “<em>unessential</em>”.</li>
  <li><strong>Essential Amino Acids (EAAs)</strong> are amino acids that aren’t made by your body, which instead
    need to be consumed as part of your diet. 9 of the 20 amino acids are “<em>essential</em>”.</li>
</ul>
<p>3 of those 9 EAAs are categorised as <em>Branched-Chain Amino Acids</em> – or BCAAs for short. <a href="#leucine" title="Studies on BCAA’s role in muscle building" target="_blank" rel="noopener">These have
    been found</a> to be particularly important for building muscle.</p>
<p><strong>Leucine</strong> is the most powerful BCAA, and the single most important nutrient for building muscle.
  There is a <a href="#leucine" title="Studies on leucine’s role in muscle building" target="_blank" rel="noopener">lot of
    research</a> demonstrating the importance of leucine in muscle building. </p>
<p>By definition, EAAs need to be consumed as part of your diet. And if there were any part of your diet you would
  expect to get your EAAs, it would be your protein supplements. </p>
<p>Here’s the thing: <strong>the <em>type</em> and <em>amount</em> of amino acids will depend on the type of
    protein.</strong></p>
<p>Not all protein was made equal. 10g of protein from eggs is going to have a completely different amino acid
  composition than 10g of protein from spinach.</p>
<p>As a visual example, the graph below compares the EAA content of whey protein vs rice protein. The data was
  taken from <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23782948" target="_blank" rel="noopener" title="The Effects of 8 Weeks of Whey or Rice Protein Supplementation on Body Composition and Exercise Performance">this
    study</a> comparing whey protein to rice protein.</p>
<picture class="intrinsic intrinsic--55">
  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/whey-protein-vs-rice-protein.png" alt="Graph comparing the essential amino acid content of whey protein versus rice protein." width="700" height="385" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</picture>
<p class="figurecap"> Essential Amino Acid Composition of Whey Protein vs Rice Protein </p>
<p>Whey protein is higher in all three BCAAs, and overall has a higher EAA content for 6 of the 9 EAAs.</p>
<p>4 of these 6 EAAs – Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, and Threonine – are <em>a lot</em> higher in whey protein than
  they are in rice protein.</p>
<p>This translates to more EAA bang for your buck per gram of whey protein versus rice protein. The source of the
  protein makes a difference.</p>
<p>Hence we have to consider more than just the pure protein content of a protein powder. The amino acid
  composition—particularly the BCAA and leucine content—should also be taken into account.</p>
<h4 id="speed">Speed Of Digestion</h4>
<p>This is how long it takes for your protein to be digested. </p>
<p>An example of this is whey protein vs casein protein. Both of these proteins are derived from milk – milk
  protein being 20% whey protein and 80% casein protein. </p>
<p><strong><em>Whey protein</em></strong> is digested rapidly – reaching peaking amino acid levels after an hour,
  and taking about 4 hours to fully digest.</p>
<p><strong><em>Casein protein</em></strong> takes longer – the amino acid levels don’t peak as high, but are
  sustained for over 7 hours. </p>
<p>This doesn’t matter as much as most supplement companies would have you believe.</p>
<p>This is because <strong><em>it’s a mistake to consider the consumption of your protein supplements in a
      vacuum</em></strong>. </p>
<p>It’s true that casein protein won’t pass through your body as quickly as whey protein. But this neglects the
  fact that you’ll have: </p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Protein flowing through your veins from other sources</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Other food slowing the digestion of your protein</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>For instance, many recommend that you consume casein protein – as opposed to whey protein – before bed. The
  logic is to allow the protein to digest slowly while you sleep.</p>
<p>In a vacuum, this sounds reasonable. </p>
<p>But you don’t live in a vacuum – you had steak and veggies for dinner. Or a similar mixed meal of fats,
  carbohydrates, and protein. </p>
<p>So there will already be protein flowing through your body from that meal. This makes the speed of digestion a
  non-factor. </p>
<p>This is reflected in the literature, with the majority of <a href="#whey-vs-casein" title="Studies on whey protein versus casein protein" target="_blank" rel="noopener">studies finding no significant
    difference</a> between whey protein and casein protein’s ability to enhance body composition.</p>
<p>That means that the “<em>slow digesting</em>” nature of a protein isn’t an important factor of consideration.
</p>
<p>Well then, what about the other side of the coin: <strong>digesting <em>really fast</em></strong>.</p>
<p><strong id="whey-hydrolysate">Whey hydrolysate</strong> jumps to mind as the fan-favourite example. Whey
  hydrolysate is taking whey protein refinement to the next level, breaking down some of the amino acid bonds by
  exposing them to heat, acids, or enzymes.</p>
<p>Effectively whey hydrolysate is whey protein that has been broken down into smaller molecules for quicker
  absorption. This pre-digestion makes hydrolysed proteins more rapidly absorbed than whey concentrates or
  isolates. </p>
<p>The story behind their benefit sounds believable: this <strong><em>lightning-fast digestion</em></strong>
  allows you to deliver a <strong><em>muscle-building dose</em></strong> of protein straight to your biceps
  post-workout. </p>
<p>Sounds sexy right?</p>
<p>Slight issue though – <strong>it doesn’t work like that</strong>. </p>
<p>For two main reasons: </p>
<ol class="ol-pad">
  <li class="li-pad"><strong>The post-workout anabolic window has been <a class="anchor" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23360586" title="Nutrient timing revisited: is there a post-exercise anabolic window?" target="_blank" rel="noopener">revealed to be irrelevant to most lifters.</a></strong>
  </li>
  <li class="li-pad"><strong>Whey hydrolysate has been shown to be <em>less effective</em> in building
      muscle.</strong>
  </li>
</ol>
<p>That’s right. </p>
<p><a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0958694613001787" title="Hydrolysed whey protein reduces muscle damage markers in Brazilian elite soccer players compared with whey protein and maltodextrin. A twelve-week in-championship intervention" target="_blank" rel="noopener">In a 2013 study</a>, athletes consumed either whey protein concentrate (WPC),
  whey protein hydrolysate (WPH), or pure sugar. </p>
<p>The scientists found that WPH decreased muscle damage more than either WPC or the sugar.</p>
<p>This sounds like something we would want, <strong>but it’s not</strong>. </p>
<p>Since muscle damage is actually necessary for muscle growth to occur, WPH had a <strong>negative
    effect</strong> on body composition. </p>
<p>The study found that <strong>WPH resulted in 65% less muscle gain than the WPC.</strong></p>
<p>And if that wasn’t bad enough: the WPH resulted in <strong>fat <em>gain</em> instead of fat
    <em>loss</em></strong>. The WPC and sugar didn’t. </p>
<p><strong><em>So the WPH was found to be more fattening than a shake of sugar.</em></strong></p>
<p>Don’t pay more for less. More research needs to be done on whey protein hydrolysate before anyone can promote
  it as “<em>superior</em>” for muscle building. For now, it’s all hype.</p>
<p>Overall this means that your protein’s speed of digestion – whether it be super fast or super slow – is not a
  good reason to pay more. </p>
<p>Hence the speed of digestion has not been included in the <a href="#table" title="Protein Comparison Table"><strong>Protein Comparison Table</strong></a>.</p>
<h4 id="price-and-taste">Price And Taste</h4>
<p>Unless you’re a billionaire without taste buds, price and taste matter.</p>
<p>In an ideal world they wouldn’t – the best protein powder would be the healthiest and most pure, irrespective
  of its taste or price tag. That’s not the world we live on though (<em>not to mention that I like my taste buds.
    I’ll take the cash though</em>).</p>
<p>Thankfully in the realm of protein powder, you don’t need to break the bank to afford effective protein
  supplements.</p>
<p>It’s often the opposite: many of the more expensive forms of protein are actually <strong>less
    effective</strong>.</p>
<p>(same goes with many of the products in a good <a href="/supplements/" title="Muscle Building Supplement Stack">muscle building
    supplement stack</a>).</p>
<p>This higher cost is typically the result of large advertising campaigns, and the ridiculous refinement
  processes required to extract the protein from this week’s trendy superfood.</p>
<p>So don’t make the mistake of thinking more expensive = more effective. It isn’t.</p>
<p><strong>Taste</strong> varies a lot depending on the <a href="#type-of-protein" title="Different Types Of Protein"><em><strong>type of protein</strong></em></a> and the <a href="#brand-of-protein" title="Different Brands Of Protein"><em><strong>brand of protein</strong></em></a>.
</p>
<p>For the <strong><em>type of protein</em></strong>, milk-derived protein powders tend to taste the best. </p>
<p>But you’ll see the largest variation in taste between different <strong><em>brands of protein</em></strong>.
</p>
<p>The quality and quantity of sweetener(s) supplement companies use result in very different tasting protein.
  Even when they’re the exact same type of protein. </p>
<p>Taste-wise, the brand makes all the difference.</p>
<p>So before you order 10kg of protein powder from your chosen supplier, make sure you check the reviews online.
  If the company sells sample packs, order one of those first before you commit to a bulk purchase.</p>

<h3 id="notes">Notes for Nerds</h3>
<h5 id="">On BCAA and Leucine Content</h5>
<p>Different studies report slightly different values for the amino acid profile of various proteins. Regardless,
  the relative differences between proteins (e.g. leucine in whey protein vs leucine in rice protein) remain
  largely the same.</p>
<h5 id="">On Slow vs Fast Digesting Protein</h5>
<p>While there are a few studies that show statistically significant differences in muscle gain when comparing
  slow versus fast digesting protein, these are the exception, not the rule. </p>
<p>These studies use unrealistic experimental conditions, such as fasting for 12+ hours before the workout or
  consuming <em>only</em> the protein supplement post-workout.</p>
<p>Many of these studies are funded by industries that have a vested interest in there being a difference. Even
  under these ideal conditions for differences to reveal themselves, the majority of studies show no difference.
</p>
<p>Hence the onus is on further research to strengthen the argument that the <a href="#speed" title="Speed Of Digestion">speed of digestion</a> of a protein has a practical application in muscle building
  for the average gym-goer.</p>
<p>Note that by ‘average’ I mean people who <strong><em>aren’t</em></strong> working out 2+ times per day or
  fasting for long periods of time pre or post workout.</p>
