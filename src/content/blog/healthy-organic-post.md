<p>Healthy. Organic. Gluten free. Fat free. No Added Sugar. Do these labels sound familiar? </p>
<p>They’re a great way for a company to sincerely inform you of the positive aspects of their product. </p>
<p>But more often than not, they’re used to mislead you into believing that what you’re buying is somehow
  “<em>good for you</em>”. </p>
<p>This leads to problems if you’re not aware of <em>exactly</em> what a claim means.</p>
<p>Let’s look at a typical example:</p>
<ul>
  <li>Stacey has just started a new diet, and is browsing her local supermarket</li>
  <li>Stacey buys a pack of nut bars that claim to be a <em>“healthy snack”</em></li>
  <li>Every time Stacey gets hungry between meals, she nibbles on a nut bar</li>
  <li>2 weeks and a couple boxes later, Stacey is 2 pounds heavier</li>
</ul>
<p>This can be a very frustrating experience for Stacey, who was under the impression that replacing her favourite
  chocolate with this <em>“healthy snack”</em> would help her lose weight. </p>
<p>Stacey’s weight loss woes can be attributed to clever marketing blended with a lack consumer understanding.
  Like many people, Stacey has associated the word <em>“healthy”</em> to <em>“low calorie”</em>. <strong><em>But
      “healthy” and “low calorie” do not mean the same thing.</em></strong> </p>
<p>Food is usually deemed <em>healthy</em> (or <em>“good for you”</em>) if it has a lot of <a href="https://www.nal.usda.gov/fnic/vitamins-and-minerals" target="_blank" title="Micronutrient defined" rel="noopener">micronutrients</a> (i.e. vitamins &amp; minerals), regardless of its <a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/public-health-nutrition/article/macronutrients-as-sources-of-food-energy/DDF89DE14E92D39D56A45FBAA3F00A55" target="_blank" title="Macronutrient defined" rel="noopener">macronutrient</a> content. Macronutrients are fat, protein and
  carbohydrates. They define the calorie content of your food, and hence the success of your weight loss efforts.
</p>
<p>If Stacey were brave enough to flip the box and read the nutrition label, she would find that these
  <em>healthy</em> nut bars have a <em>lot</em> of fat in them.
</p>
<p>Although many would argue these to be <em>“healthy fats”</em>, this does not relieve them of their high calorie
  content – easily leading to unwanted weight gain if you’re not careful with your portion sizes.</p>
<p>Note that the supplier hasn’t actually lied about their product. Nuts are <em>healthy</em>. It’s just that food
  can be <em>healthy</em> without being low in calories – nuts being a prime example. </p>
<p>The confusion between <a href="/blog/healthy-low-calorie-foods" target="_blank" title="Difference between Healthy and Low Calorie" rel="noopener"><em>healthy</em> and <em>low calorie</em> foods</a> can
  lead people to believe that a product is something it’s not.</p>

<p>Nut bars aren’t evil. </p>
<p>They’re just one of the many products who are a regular target of disingenuous marketing.</p>
<p>Candy is another common target, which frequently boasts about having <em>“No Artificial Colours or
    Flavours”</em> or being <em>“99% Fat Free”</em>. </p>
<p>The number of people who read these labels and think <em>“These are good for me”</em> is concerning. Once
  familiar with this type of advertising, you’ll instead instantly conclude: <em>“Made with 100% Sugar”</em>.</p>
<p>Now I’m not saying that sugar is the devil and you should avoid it at all costs – because it definitely isn’t
  and you definitely shouldn’t. </p>
<p><strong><em>But when you indulge on a sweet treat it’s important to be aware that it is just that – a sweet
      treat</em></strong>.</p>
<p>Otherwise there’ll be no guilt holding you back from heading down to your local McDonalds and ordering a big
  mac and fries for round 2. I mean, why not – you’ve eaten so well today! </p>
<p>An eating habit like this can easily lead to unwanted weight gain.</p>

  <img class="intrinsic-item" src="/img/majin-buu-eating.gif" alt="Eating cake" width="500" height="280" loading="lazy" decoding="async">

<p class="figurecap"> Don’t worry – it’s Gluten Free </p>
<p>This isn’t purely a marketing problem. </p>
<p>If a candy company wants to accurately label its product as having <em>“No Artificial Colours or
    Flavours”</em>, they should be able to. That way those looking to buy candy without artificial colours or
  flavours will be able to easily identify what products they can choose from.</p>
<p>To fix this issue long term, we need to bridge the gap in consumer knowledge. <em>“No Artificial Colours or
    Flavours”</em> on a pack of candy should not distract you from the candy’s 100% sugar content.</p>
<p>Although it would be convenient, it’s unrealistic to expect a company to actively market such information.
  Until crooked teeth become fashionable, we probably won’t see:</p>
<blockquote>
  <p>“No artificial colours or flavours. Boatloads of sugar.”</p>
  <p>— Nestlé </p>
</blockquote>
<p>The underlying issue is the <a title="Information asymmetry defined." target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_asymmetry" rel="noopener"><strong><em>information asymmetry</em></strong></a>
  between suppliers and consumers. Suppliers know exactly what’s in their products. Consumers don’t. </p>
<p>So a consumer must read and interpret a product’s labelling. But <strong><em>the goal of most labelling is to
      sell, not to inform</em></strong>. So a consumer must be able to correctly interpret what a label (or
  buzzword) means.</p>
<p>Although the average consumer is better at reading a label than they used to be, that doesn’t say much. </p>
<p>I’ve known people who thought a product was <em>“healthy”</em> purely because it was labelled <em>“Low in
    salt!”</em> – which is what I imagine a marketer’s wet dream to be like.</p>
<p>Not to forget: </p>
<ul>
  <li>High sugar cereals that justify being good for kids because of the vitamins and minerals they smuggle into
    the ingredients list.</li>
  <li><em>“No Added Cane Sugar!”</em> drinks that swap the cane sugar for fruit sugar (fructose). </li>
  <li>The <em>“Heart Foundation Tick of Approval”</em> being slapped onto anything low in fat. </li>
</ul>
<p>With all these options available to mislead the uninformed consumer, it’s no wonder that people find proper
  nutrition to be so disheartenedly confusing.</p>
